## Knowledge 1. Standardization of Options

The listed option exchagnes have standardized the terms of option contracts.

### Knowledge 1.1 Striking Price

Striking prices are spaced at different intervals for stocks, ETFs, and indices, depending on the underlying price and the liquidity of the option contracts.

With rare exceptions, striking prices are 5 points apart at most, even for high-priced stocks like GOOG and AAPL or indices like SPX.

For lower-priced stock, strikes are often just 1 point apart, provided that the options on that stock are relatively heavily traded.

There are occasionally 2.5-point strikes as well, centered between 5-point striking price intervals.

### Knowledge 1.2 Expiration Dates

Options have expiration dates in one of three fixed cycles.

1. the January/April/July/October cycle, 
2. the February/May/August/November cycle, or
3. the March/June/September/December cycle.

In addition, the two nearest months have listed options as well.

However, at any given time, the longest-term expiration dates are normally no farther away than 9 months.

Longer-term options, also called LEAPS (Long-term equity Anticipation Securtities), are available on some stocks.

Hence, in any cycle, options may expire in 3 of the 4 major months + the near-term months.

The exact date of expiration is fixed within each month. The last trading day for an option is the third Friday in the expiration month. This expiration date is considered the "regular" option expiration date in any given month. 

But in recent years, options have been introduced that expire at other times. There are quarterly options that expire on the last trading day of March, June, September, and December. There are only a few of these - mostly likely on large indices. There are weekly options. These are generally listed on a Thursday and expire on the Friday that occurs eight calendar days later. An ever-growing number of stocks and indices have weekly listed options.

### Knowledge 1.3 The Price of an Option

Options trade in one-penny increments in many cases. Otherwise, prices are quoted in 5-cent increments. For stock options, the trading increment is one penny up until the price of the option reaches $3.00. After that, the trading increment is 5 cents. However, for liquid ETFs and indices, options can trade in pennies at any price.

## Knowledge 2. Key Definitions

### Knowledge 2.1 Classes and Series

A class of options refers to all put and call contracts on the same underlying security.

A series, a subset of a class, consists of all contracts of the same class having the same expiration date and striking price.

### Knowledge 2.2 Open Interest

Open interest (OI) in options means how many option contracts are currently open and outstanding — i.e., created but not yet closed, exercised, or expired.

| Transaction type                               | Open Interest |
|-----------------------------------------------|---------------|
| Buy ↔ Sell (new buyer, new seller)             | +1            |
| Buy ↔ Sell (existing holder exits)             | No change     |
| Closing a position (long + short offset)       | –1            |
| Expiration / Exercise                          | –1 per contract |

### Knowledge 2.3 The Holder and Writer

Anyone who buys an option as the initial transaction - that is buys opening - is called the holder. On the other hand, the investor who sells an optin as the initial transaction - an opening sale - is called the writer of the option.

## Knowledge 3. Black-Scholes Model

### Knowledge 3.1 (BS PDE)

Assume the stock $S_t$ follows a geometric Brownian motion (GBM):


$$
dS_t=\mu S_tdt+\sigma S_tdW_t
$$


where

* $\mu$: expected rate of return
* $\sigma$: volatility
* $dW_t$: increment of a Wiener process (standard Brownian motion)

Let option value be a function of $S$ and $t$:


$$
C=C(S,t)
$$


Using Itô’s lemma to expand $dC$


$$
dC=C_tdt+C_SdS+\frac{1}{2}C_{SS}(dS)^2
$$

$$
dC=(C_t+\mu SC_S+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2C_{SS})dt+\sigma SC_S dW_t
$$


Build a hedged portoflio $\Pi$ by

* Long one option
* Short $\Delta$ shares of the underlying


$$
\Pi=C-\Delta S
$$


Then 


$$
d\Pi=dC-\Delta dS
$$


Substitute $dC$ and $dS$ from above


$$
d\Pi=[C_t+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2C_{SS}+(\mu SC_S-\Delta\mu S)]dt+(\sigma SC_S-\Delta\sigma S)dW_t
$$


The risk of the portfolio comes from $dW_t$, set the coefficient of $dW_t$ to zero:


$$
\Delta=C_S
$$


A risk-free portoflio must grow at the risk-free rate $r$:


$$
d\Pi=r\Pi dt=r(C-SC_S)dt=[C_t+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2C_{SS}]dt
$$


Then we have


$$
\boxed{C_t+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2S^2C_{SS}+rSC_S-rC=0}
$$


This is the **Black-Scholes partial differential equation**.

At expiry $T$:


$$
\boxed{C(S, T)=\max{(S-K, 0)}}
$$


### Knowledge 3.2 (BS Formula)

**Call price:**


$$
C=S_0N(d_1)-Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)
$$


**Put price**:


$$
P=Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2)-S_0N(-d_1)
$$


where 

* $S_0$: current stock price

* $K$: strike price

* $r$: risk-free rate

* $T$: time to maturity

* $\sigma$: volatility of underlying

* $N(\cdot)$: standard normal CDF

* $$
  d_1=\frac{\ln{(S_0/K)+(r+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T}}{\sigma\sqrt{T}},d_2=d_1-\sigma\sqrt{T}
  $$

* ATM premium in percent is roughly $\frac{\sigma\sqrt{T}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}=0.4\sigma\sqrt{T}$ if one assumes zero interest rates

### Knowledge 3.3 (Greeks)

**Delta**


$$
\Delta_c=N(d_1), \Delta_p=N(d_1)-1
$$


**Gamma**


$$
\Gamma=\frac{N'(d_1)}{S_0\sigma\sqrt{T}}
$$


**Vega**


$$
\text{Vega}=\frac{\partial C}{\partial\sigma}=S_0N'(d_1)\sqrt{T}
$$


**Theta**

$$



**Rho**

**Vomma (Volga)**

## Knowledge 4. Intrinsic Value and Time Value

### Knowledge 4.1 (Definition)


$$
\text{Price of option = Intrinsic value + Time value}
$$

$$
\text{Intrisic value} = \max{(S-K, 0)}\text{ for call }\max{(K-S, 0)}\text{ for put}
$$

where $S$ is spot price and $K$ is strike price

* **Intrinsic Value**: how much the option is "in the money" right now
* **Time Value**: the extra premium the buyer pays for the possibility that opiton will gain value  before expiration

### Knowledge 4.2

**An ATM option has the greatest time value.**

* Intutuively, ATM options have the highest uncertainty about ending up ITM/OTM at expiry
* ATM options -> 50/50 chance, maximum gamma, and maximum sensitivity to future price movements

**Mathematicall Proof**

* Spot: $S_0$
* Strike: $K$
* Maturity: $T$
* Risk-free discount factor: $D:=e^{-rT}$
* Risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$

The price of call option is 


$$
C(K)=D\mathbb{E_Q}[(S_T-K)^+]
$$


Time value of call option is 


$$
\text{TV}(K)=C(K)-\max{(S_0-K, 0)}
$$


Then we have


$$
\frac{\partial{C(K)}}{\partial K}=D\mathbb{E_Q}[\frac{\partial(S_T-K)^+}{\partial K}]=-D\mathbb{P_Q}[S_T\geq K]
$$


When $K <S_0$


$$
\frac{\partial{\text{TV}(K)}}{\partial K}=-D\mathbb{P_Q}[S_T\geq K]+1\geq 0
$$


When $K\geq S_0$


$$
\frac{\partial{\text{TV}(K)}}{\partial K}=-D\mathbb{P_Q}[S_T\geq K]\leq 0
$$


Since $\text{TV}$ is increasing for $K<S_0$ and descreasing for $K>S_0$, it attains its maximum at $K=S_0$ (ATM).

## Knowledge 5. Delta and Probability with ITM

Call delta:


$$
\Delta_c=N(d_1)
$$


Probability of being ITM at expiry


$$
\mathbb{P}(S_T\geq K)=N(d_2)

### Knowledge 6.2 (Pinning the strike)

**Pinning** (often called *“pinning the strike”* or *“expiration pinning”*) refers to the tendency of a stock’s price to gravitate toward a nearby **option strike price** as the options on that stock **approach expiration** — especially when there is **large open interest** at that strike.

Example:
If many options on stock XYZ are written at the **$100 strike** and expiry is today, the stock often closes **very close to $100**— even if random walk dynamics would have pushed it elsewhere.

When expiry nears:

1. $\Gamma$ becomes very large for ATM options
2. Even a tiny move in price changes delta a lot
3. Dealers must trade aggressively in the underlying stock to stay hedged

Now consider both sides:

* Stock goes up slightly above strike -> Call delta increases -> Dealers sell stock to rebalance -> Stock price down toward to strike
* Stock goes down slightly below strike -> Call delta decreases -> Dealers buy stock to rebalance -> Stock price up toward to strike

## Knowledge 7. "Plain-Vanilla" Startegies

### Knowledge 7.1 (Call Overwriting)

**Call overwriting** (also called a **covered call writing**) is an options trading strategy where an investor **sells call options** on an asset they **already own**. It’s a way to **generate additional income** from holdings, while **limiting potential upside**.

* Neutral to slightly bullish outlook
* Income generation from option premiums
* Capped upside, same downside as owning the stock
* Implied volatility matters --- higher IV --- higher premiums

**Basic Idea**

You hold a stock (ETF, index) and "overwrite" it by selling a call option on it.

* You receive the option premium
* If the stock price stays below the strike, the option expires worthless --- keep the premium and shares
* If the stock price goes above the strike, must sell shares at strike price --- miss out on further upside

**When It's Used**

* Investor expect sideways or mildly bullish markets
* Used by funds to enhance yield
* Traders may systematically sell calls against long portfolios to reduce volatility

[**BXM Index**](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/indices/factsheet/CboeGlobalIndices_BXM-Index.pdf)

BXM stands for the Cboe S&P 500 BuyWrite Index.

It is a benchmark index that tracks the performance of a covered call (call overwriting) strategy on the S&P 500 Index (SPX).

Each month, the BXM index simulates this process:

1. **Hold the S&P 500** (i.e., long the index).
2. **Sell (write) one-month, at-the-money SPX call options.**
3. **Hold to expiration**, then:
   - If the call expires worthless → keep the premium.
   - If the call is exercised → sell the index at the strike (and re-enter next month).

Then it repeats monthly.

So it’s a **systematic monthly covered call strategy** — also called a **buy-write** strategy.

### Knowledge 7.2 (Put Underwriting)

**Put underwriting** (also known as a **cash-secured put** strategy) is the mirror image of call overwriting.

It means selling (writing) a put option on an asset you would like to buy, and holding enough cash to purchase it if assigned.

**Core Idea**

You’re acting like an insurance seller:

- You collect the option premium (income).
- If the price stays above the strike**, the option expires worthless — you keep the premium.
- If the price falls below the strike, you must buy the asset at the strike price — effectively at a discount.

**When It’s Used**

- You’re bullish or neutral on the stock.
- You want to accumulate a stock at a lower effective price.
- You’re comfortable owning the underlying if it drops slightly.

[**Put Index**](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/indices/factsheet/CboeGlobalIndices_PUT-Index.pdf)

PUT stands for the Cboe S&P 500 PutWrite Index.

It measures the performance of a systematic cash-secured put-selling strategy on the S&P 500 Index (SPX).

Each month, the PUT index simulates this rule-based process:

1. Sell (write) one-month, at-the-money SPX put options.
2. Hold cash collateral equal to the strike price × option multiplier (so it’s *cash-secured*).
3. At expiration:
   - If SPX > strike → option expires worthless → keep the premium.
   - If SPX < strike → option exercised → buy SPX exposure at strike (and adjust for next cycle).
4. Repeat monthly.

So the PUT index earns:

- Premium income each month,
- Minus losses when the index falls below the strike.

## Knowledge 8. GEX (Gamma Exposure)

### Knowledge 8.1 Definition

**Gamma Exposure (GEX)** is the estimated net gamma position held by options **market makers** across all strikes and expirations. It measures how much a dealer’s total delta changes for every 1% move in the underlying — and therefore, how aggressively they must buy or sell to stay hedged as price moves.

**Assumption**: Investors and large funds lower risk and protect their money by selling calls and buying puts. Market makers provide the liquidity to facilitate these trades. GEX assumes that market makers are part of every transaction and that the bulk of their transactions are buying calls and selling puts to investors hedging their portfolios.

Market makers profit from the bid-ask spreads and as such, they constantly gamma hedge (they buy and sell shares to keep their positions delta neutral).

Long call positions are positive gamma - as the stock price increases and delta rises (approaches 1), market makers hedge by selling shares, and they buy shares if the stock price decreases and delta falls.

Short put positions are negative gamma - as the stock price increases and delta falls (approaches -1), market makers hedge by buying shares, and they sell shares if the stock price decreases and delta rises.

As such, in the event of large positive gamma, volatility is suppressed as market makers will hedge by buying as the stock price decreases and selling as the stock price increases. And in the event of large negative gamma, volatility is amplified as market makers will hedge by buying as the stock price increases and selling as the stock price decreases.
